# 19: Device-to-device sync

**Status**: normative for the decisions it records. R74's device identity, enrolment, revocation, issuer rotation, the client's half of the clock rule and the lost-key path are built and marked **Built (R74)**, and R76's link, discovery, the Android hosting and joining, the beacon and the exchange, the text and QR fallback, the iPhone host and iPad join, the Linux hosting and joining and the Mac join are built and marked **Built (R76)**. Every other statement carries **Decided (RN)**, where `RN` is the phase in `plans/master-implementation-plan.md` that builds it. The design was settled and adversarially reviewed on 2026-08-21 and derived into phases R74 to R80 on 2026-08-22. The R25 section of the plan records every decision with its rejected alternatives, and the review that amended the design in place.

---

## Purpose

**Decided (R25).** Several people collaborate offline on tasks. The driving case is sample collection in the wild: a team in the Amazon basin with zero internet, mixed iPhone and Android fleets, camera media, and days of irreplaceable observations. Their devices hold overlapping replicas and today cannot exchange anything, because every path assumes the server: the exactly-once watermark is keyed on a server-minted handle, cursors are positions in the server's change log, and change-path authorization is answered server-side.

## Semantics: provisional, the server adjudicates

**Decided (R25).** What peers build offline is provisional, and the server still adjudicates when connectivity returns. The alternative, a peer-authoritative CRDT mesh with the server as just another peer, was rejected because it replaces server-wins, the single Postgres-authoritative log and the change-path authorization model, and no surveyed system keeps a logical-replication core under one.

Stated so nobody oversells it: an offline edit can be reversed later by the server's verdict, a right the local policy cannot answer blocks the write instead of guessing, two people editing the same row offline get one locally visible version with the displaced author recorded (one row per key exists, the server orders the truth later), and nothing here serves collaboration between strangers who were never online together with the same deployment.

## Trust: device identity and certificates

**Decided (R74, 2026-10-02).** While online and signed in, a native device creates a P-256 key inside its security chip (the Secure Enclave, Android Keystore or Windows TPM, which never release it, and a software key in the device's secret store where no usable chip exists), proves possession with a certificate request carrying a server nonce, and the server's issuer signs an X.509 certificate binding the key to the account. One certificate per account on the device, matching R42's model. The deployment's root key stays offline and signs replaceable issuers, and the application is built with the root it expects, so a device never adopts a root it is handed. The server verifies attestation at enrolment where a device offers it and records one of three levels in the certificate, `chip-proven` when Android attests the key in its TEE or StrongBox, `app-attested` when Apple's App Attest vouches for the app that asked, and `unproven` otherwise, and each deployment decides which levels to refuse. The key signs once the device has been unlocked since its last restart, so peers exchange with their screens locked, and the issuer is replaced yearly under a 10-year root. Browsers never enrol (R75).

The lifetime is application-requested per case under a server ceiling and refused if over, the R4 pattern. The ceiling defaults to 30 days and is configurable, an unspecified request gets 24 hours, renewal auto-runs on any connectivity past half-life, and a connected client can request a new certificate at another lifetime. There is no human pairing ceremony (the CA and the policy check replace Syncthing-style fingerprint exchange) and no offline enrolment or sub-issuance (a server CA cannot mint in the field, and delegation is attack surface for marginal gain), so a device that never enrolled is a spectator until it meets the server once.

**Decided (R74, 2026-10-02), lost-device notices travel between peers.** Reporting a device lost revokes its certificates and its sessions, and the issuer publishes a signed X.509 revocation list numbered per issuer. Devices receive lists from the server and hand them to one another inside every authenticated link, keep the highest-numbered list per issuer, and refuse a reported device once any list naming it reaches them. A group no list reaches trusts the certificate until it expires, so lifetime bounds the offline revocation lag. The lost-device list shows a descriptor the application defines, for display only.

**Decided (R74, 2026-09-12 and 2026-10-02), the clock rule and the lost-key path.** Certificate validity is checked against the wall clock with five minutes of tolerance at both ends, the one wall-clock check in the tree a peer cannot correct against a server. A device whose clock puts every certificate outside its window fails closed on every peer link and surfaces one typed event naming the cause, so an expedition device whose clock drifted off-grid is told why it sees no peers. A device whose custody record is gone, by a cleared keychain or a removed store, enrols again with a fresh key exactly as a first enrolment, and the dangling enrolment expires at its lifetime. Provisional rows peers hold under the old key are adjudicated or retracted by the server's frontier like any other author's, since the device cannot sign for a key it no longer holds. On Linux the device key shares the replica key's custody, which survives a reboot (R71).

**Built (R74, 2026-10-04), the device key.** A native build on the platform keyring holds one P-256 key per account, usable once the device was unlocked since its restart and never prompted for. It lives in the Secure Enclave on an iPhone, iPad or Mac whose build carries the keychain entitlement, in the Android Keystore's trusted environment, and in the TPM through the Microsoft Platform Crypto Provider on Windows. A device with no usable chip, which covers Linux, a Mac build without the entitlement and a Windows machine without a usable TPM, keeps a software key as a reserved record in its keyring, outside the unlock gate. An Android application hands in its JNI access with `NativeDurable::with_java_access`, which `connetto_auth_session::java_access()` provides for a Dioxus application, and `NativeClient::device_key_home()` reports where the key lives beside `custody()`. A build whose stores the application names has no device identity.

**Built (R74, 2026-10-04), enrolment.** A task beside the client's pump enrols on every connection that finds no certificate, renews past half-life, and serves `NativeClient::reissue_certificate`. It asks for a single-use 60-second nonce, signs a certificate request carrying it on the key's own thread, and sends it with the lifetime and the application's descriptor (`with_certificate_lifetime`, `with_device_descriptor`). The server issues an X509-SVID leaf under its current issuer and records the enrolment and the serial before it answers. A lifetime over the ceiling is refused, and a renewal refused that way is asked again at the ceiling with `ClientEvent::CertificateLifetimeCapped`. The application ships its deployment roots through `with_deployment_roots`, a build with a device identity and no roots does not connect, and every granted chain must reach one of them. The certificate is kept in the replica's `_connetto_device_certificate` and is readable as soon as `connect` returns. Every wait on the server ends after 30 seconds and at once on disconnect.

**Built (R74, 2026-10-04), revocation.** The owner reports a device lost from any of the account's sessions with `NativeClient::revoke_device`, and the operator through `SessionManager::revoke_device`. The server lists every serial of the key whose certificate has not been expired longer than `DEVICE_SKEW` (R72, chapter 21) under the issuer's next CRL Number, closes the device's connections with `FatalErrorReason::DeviceRevoked`, pushes the new lists to every identified session, and revokes the auth-store session its enrolment last ran under through the deployment's session revoker. Every identified session also receives the current lists right after its handshake, and every grant carries them. A client keeps per signer the verified list with the highest number in `_connetto_revocation_list`, ignores a lower one, and logs and ignores an equal number with other content. A device that finds its own serial listed, or is closed as revoked, deletes its key and certificate and emits `ClientEvent::DeviceRevoked`. `NativeClient::devices` returns the account's devices with each descriptor read back as the application's type. Revoking a key that is already revoked repeats every side effect, the published list, the closed connection and the revoked session, so a report whose list failed to publish is completed by asking again (decision 36).

**Built (R74, 2026-10-04), issuer rotation.** A retired issuer stays in the server's configuration (`DeviceCertConfig::with_retired_issuer`) and signs the list of its own certificates until it expires. `connetto-ca revoke-issuer` keeps the root's one complete list of revoked issuers in the CA directory and signs it again under the next number, and the server publishes it beside the issuers' lists (`with_root_list`). A device whose own issuer that list names deletes its certificate, keeps its key, emits `ClientEvent::CertificateWithdrawn`, and enrols the same key under the current issuer on its next connection.

**Built (R74, 2026-10-05), the enrolment tables.** The server records enrolments in three tables of the deployment's own, named by the `Enrolments` member of its `ConnettoSchema`: one row per device key with its account, session, times and descriptor, one row per issued certificate with its serial, key, issuer and expiry, and one row per issuer holding its last list number, advanced in one atomic upsert so numbers rise across restarts and server instances. The descriptor's columns map the application's own type, which `connetto_schema!` destructures field by field, so a field list that disagrees with it does not compile, and an enrolment whose descriptor does not decode into it is refused with `InvalidRequest`. `ServerBuilder::device_identity` turns enrolment on, requires the three tables at startup and revokes a lost device's session in the auth store. The binary reads the issuer from the directory `connetto-ca issuer` wrote (chapter 20).

**Built (R74, 2026-10-05), the clock rule on the client and the lost-key path.** A client learns its clock is wrong from its own certificate. A certificate the server just granted that the local clock already puts outside its window, or one held at open that is not yet valid by more than five minutes, moves it to `ClockOff` and raises `ClientEvent::ClockOutsideWindow { ahead }` once, the server's issuance serving as the trusted time. While `ClockOff` the hourly look re-evaluates the window and never renews, and a connection still renews once, which leaves the state when the new certificate falls inside the window. A device whose key record is gone opens a fresh key, drops the certificate of the lost one and enrols the fresh key as a first enrolment, and the old enrolment stays until it expires.

**Built (R74, 2026-10-05), attestation.** A device's first enrolment carries its platform's evidence, and the server records one of three levels in the enrolment table and in a non-critical certificate extension, which renewals keep. An Android key is created with the fixed attestation challenge `connetto device key` and sends its Keystore chain, which the server checks against Google's attestation roots and Google's attestation status list, recording `chip-proven` for a `TrustedEnvironment` or `StrongBox` key. A chain naming another key or challenge is refused, and every other shortfall, the emulator's software-rooted chain among them, records `unproven`. An iPhone or iPad asks App Attest to sign over the request's hash through `connetto-app-attest`, the workspace's one crate allowed `unsafe`, which serialises every App Attest call behind one process-wide lock since Apple's first call is not thread-safe, and the server checks it against Apple's root and the App IDs the deployment lists, recording `app-attested`. Windows, Linux and a Mac record `unproven`. A deployment names the levels it accepts, all three by default, and refuses the rest with `AttestationRequired`, which the client raises as `ClientEvent::AttestationRequired` and asks again only on its next connection.

**Built (R74, 2026-10-15 and 2026-10-18), the Apple attestations.** An iPhone 15 Pro Max enrols as `app-attested` on hardware, with a real attestation from it checked in as the verifier's fixture (#146), and the Wi-Fi iPad A16 enrolled as `app-attested` against a server accepting no lower level. **Decided (R74, R76), what the built part waits on.** Windows and Linux have not yet recorded `unproven` on hardware, which R76's home run produces.

## One exactly-once domain: the per-device applied frontier

**Decided (R75).** Every write carries its durable identity, the pair of its writer and its pending-queue sequence. A native device with an enrolled key writes as that key, and every other client (a browser profile, a build without a device identity, a device before its first enrolment, a caller with no identity) writes under a number the server issues, since browsers never join a peer link and no browser API offers an application a chip-held signing key (decided 2026-10-02 and 2026-10-17). A connection proves its device writer once, by signing the nonce the server opens every connection with. The server keeps one frontier per writer below which every sequence is settled, applied or refused, and every path takes only the next sequence. The normal reconnect path, the courier path and an archive restore all settle through it, and the per-connection watermark is a cache of it.

The server keeps a record of each settled write, its chain hash, its outcome and the path that settled it, for as long as its writer can still deliver it. A device writer's records go once its enrolment is revoked or has gone absent, an expired certificate keeping them behind a same-key renewal for the replay and fork evidence, and a number writer's once the author reports it has settled past them. A re-delivery is answered from that record, and audited when it arrives through another path than the one that settled it. A different entry at a settled (writer, sequence) is refused and audited, first-seen-wins, the Secure Scuttlebutt fork rule, adopted after the review mapped SSB's known fork condition onto archive restore. An archive import therefore always retires the archived writer and gives the importing replica a fresh one, with restored entries carrying their original identity as provenance so the frontier deduplicates the logical writes across the restore. A device whose writer an import retired carries its own unsent entries forward the same way. The pending queue holds every unsent write and evicts none, so every provisional copy a peer holds stays confirmable or retractable, and every entry chains to the one before it, so a range a device signs vouches for everything below it.

Outstanding writes have durable append positions across writers. Reapplication and archive export follow those positions, which carries preserve. Older number and unissued tails drain before device binding, and the archive's pending list preserves their causal order.

Device retirement commits the carried tail and a durable key-exclusion marker together before custody cleanup and fresh enrolment. Device revocation uses the same queue transaction and then deletes custody through its revocation lifecycle, without requesting writer-retirement enrolment. A held or renewed certificate cannot reactivate an excluded writer.

## Transport

**Decided (R76).** The data plane is the local network, with discovery by gateway-probe first (a hotspot host IS the gateway, so clients need no multicast to find it) and mDNS as the general case under the service type `_connetto-peer._tcp.local`, then mutual TLS over plain TCP with both sides verifying against the built-in roots and the revocation lists. It is for native targets only, phones, tablets, laptops and desktops alike, since browsers cannot take the listening role on any radio path and keep the server path. The TXT record carries the protocol version and a certificate fingerprint only, never an account identity, and discovery is treated as fully public with authentication happening only at the handshake.

**Built (R76, 2026-10-07), the link.** The `connetto-peer` crate holds the link, and `connetto-client`'s `peer` feature drives it from the enrolment task. A device holding a valid certificate listens at the address `NativeDurable::with_peer_listener` names, `0.0.0.0:0` by default, and dials with `NativeClient::link_peer`. Both sides run TLS 1.3 signed by their device keys, verify each other against the deployment roots, the kept revocation lists, the five-minute window and the accepted attestation levels, and then exchange the lists the other lacks through the same intake the server's pushes use, so a list learned from a peer revokes exactly as one pushed by the server. A link pings every 15 seconds, closes after 45 seconds of silence or when the peer's certificate expires, takes a renewed certificate in a `Certificate` frame, and two devices dialling each other keep one link. A device whose certificate expires, whose clock leaves the window, or whose certificate is withdrawn or revoked closes every link and stops listening. The Bluetooth exchange is the next slice.

**Built (R76, 2026-10-07), discovery.** While a device serves, it advertises `_connetto-peer._tcp.local` over mDNS under a random name, with only the frame version and the SHA-256 of its current certificate in the TXT record, and browses for the others, reporting each as `ClientEvent::PeerFound` and `PeerGone`. Unless the application turns autolink off with `NativeDurable::with_peer_autolink(false)`, the client dials what it finds, retrying an unreachable peer from 5 seconds up to 5 minutes and never redialling a refused, revoked or expired one until it advertises a renewed certificate. At most 8 dials run at once and at most 256 found devices are tracked, so fake advertisements cannot exhaust the device. Android holds a multicast lock while browsing. A device that joins a hotspot through the offer or the text fallback dials the host directly at the gateway, which arrives with the hotspot slice.

**Built (R76, 2026-10-08), hosting and joining on Android.** `NativeClient::host_hotspot` starts Android's local-only hotspot and answers its SSID, passphrase and, while the device serves, its peer port. `join_hotspot` asks the platform for that network, which the user approves, binds only the peer sockets to it so the server connection keeps the phone's usual network, and dials the host at the gateway on the offered port. The hotspot follows the application's calls and the client's life, not the certificate, so an expired host keeps its group's network up. The Kotlin these need ships in `connetto-peer-android`, and every system whose slice has not landed answers `Unsupported`. On the two A35s the Android 15 phone hosted and the Android 14 phone joined and linked. An A35 started its access point only once its own Wi-Fi was off, measured while that Wi-Fi sat on eduroam's 5 GHz channel 56.

**Built (R76, 2026-10-17), the state reads.** The client's event stream is broadcast, so an application that keeps a list from its events holds a wrong one after any dropped event. The node's read `Node::linked` stands the identities of every peer it reports linked, live slots and a duplicate close's held peer alike, and `NativeClient::linked_peers` hands them to the application, an empty list for a build without a device. `NativeClient::nearby_hosts` stands the scan's hosts as `NearbyHost` values with their host key, beacon prefix and last signal, published whenever a host is seen, refreshed, renamed or gone, and the test harness's Linux leg reads both instead of keeping its own standing.

**Decided (R76, 2026-10-02), making a shared network and joining it.** Android, Windows and Linux start a hotspot from the application (Android's local-only hotspot, a Wi-Fi Direct access point on Windows, NetworkManager's hotspot on Linux). macOS cannot host from an application, and an iPhone or iPad hosts only through Personal Hotspot switched on by hand, whose password the user types into the application once. A host announces itself with a public Bluetooth beacon carrying the protocol version and its certificate fingerprint. A joining device connects to it over Bluetooth, both run the same mutual TLS as the Wi-Fi link with the same checks and exchange revocation lists, and only then does the host send the hotspot's name and password, encrypted for that device, as Apple's Wi-Fi password sharing proves identity before sending a password. With Bluetooth off, the host shows a `WIFI:` QR code and the same details as text. Every platform then asks its own system to join.

The decision rests on these field facts. A hotspot host is a member of its own network, Android at `192.168.x.1` through `LocalOnlyHotspot`, application-startable with no SIM service, and iOS at `172.20.10.1` through a manual, carrier-gated toggle whose zero-coverage start stays unverified until R80's field test. Android multicast reception needs a `MulticastLock`, and iOS 14 and later and macOS 15 and later prompt once for local-network permission. A university network can put devices in separate subnets that mDNS cannot cross (measured on eduroam), and a battery travel router removes every hotspot caveat. Bluetooth carries only the hotspot handoff. A full Bluetooth data plane is never promised, the crate landscape being pre-1.0 and single-maintainer, and Noise returns to the cipher table only if that plane ever exists.

**Built (R76, 2026-10-09), the beacon and the exchange.** The `bluetooth` feature of `connetto-peer` advertises the beacon while the device both hosts a hotspot and serves its peer link. The beacon codec (`beacon.rs`) carries the frame version and the fingerprint of the device's current certificate in a legacy advertisement, and the framed exchange (`exchange.rs`) runs the link's TLS 1.3 with the same verifier over a GATT service under connetto's service UUID, one characteristic the joiner writes and one it reads notifications from, the frames split to the negotiated packet size. Every exchange is bounded at 30 seconds, at most 4 run at once on a host, and a fifth connection is refused at once. The host swaps `Hello` frames and the lists either side lacks into the one intake and only then does its `Node::offer_over` send the `HotspotOffer` with its peer port, and the joiner's `NativeClient::fetch_offer` reads the same offer. On Android the host runs `BluetoothGattServer`. On emi one A35 hosted with its beacon in its own Bluetooth dump, and the exchange and both state machines were proven over in-memory transports.

**Built (R76, 2026-10-09), the join side.** `connetto-client` runs btleplug 0.13.5 on one dedicated thread as its central (`BtleplugCentral` in `crates/connetto-client/src/bluetooth/central.rs`), attached to the application's class loader, with btleplug's Java in `connetto-peer-android`'s Gradle module and `java_vm` building the running virtual machine from the one manganis hands out. `join_nearby` runs the exchange with a found host and hands the offer to the platform's joiner, and `fetch_offer` asks a named host for its offer without joining. On emi the Android 14 A35 saw the Android 15 one's beacon on its panel, joined through the demo's `Join the nearby host`, the exchange handing over the offer, the runner tapping the approval, and both phones named each other linked.

**Built (R76, 2026-10-09), the text and QR fallback.** `HotspotOffer::to_wifi_payload` writes the standard `WIFI:` fields with the peer port in the extension field, `WIFI:T:<WPA or SAE>;S:<ssid>;P:<password>;X:<port>;;` with the format's backslash escapes, and `from_wifi_payload` reads it with a typed refusal (`WifiPayloadError`), so a phone's system camera joins the Wi-Fi and skips the port while an application that scans the code or takes the string learns the port and dials the gateway at once. The offer's three lines beside the payload are the text form. On emi the Android 15 A35 hosted with its Bluetooth off, the runner declined the system's enable dialog, and the Android 14 one joined from the pasted payload and linked.

**Built (R76, 2026-10-12), the iPhone host and the iPad join.** `connetto-peer-apple` bridges `CBPeripheralManager`, `NEHotspotConfiguration` and `UIDevice`, `connetto-client` adds `AppleBluetoothBackend` and `AppleHotspotBackend`, and the btleplug central runs over CoreBluetooth. Hosting is `host_personal_hotspot(ssid, passphrase)`, both values typed by the caller since iOS gives no application its Personal Hotspot's password, and `personal_hotspot_name()` answers the real network name where the application holds Apple's device-name entitlement and `None` elsewhere, which the demo's form prefills. The iPhone's beacon advertises connetto's service UUID alone, since Apple lets an application's advert carry a local name and service UUIDs only, and a joiner keys such a host by its Bluetooth address, which iOS rotates, so autojoin tries a reappeared host once more. An offer no joiner lands on reports `ClientEvent::HotspotUnreached`, the beacon keeps advertising, and the retry belongs to the people involved. An Apple joiner applies its configuration once more when the system answers success but the interface has not moved, once per join within the join bound. On 2026-10-12 the iPhone 15 Pro Max on iOS 26.6 hosted and the iPad A16 joined by beacon, pasted payload and typed form, each linking over the hotspot, in one unattended run.

**Built (R76, 2026-10-13), hosting and joining on Linux.** `connetto-peer`'s Linux module drives NetworkManager over zbus and runs the beacon's GATT service over BlueZ through `bluer`, and `connetto-client` adds `LinuxHotspotBackend` and `LinuxBluetoothBackend` beside the btleplug central. A Linux host names its network `connetto-` and four random hex digits with a fresh passphrase held in memory, tries 5 GHz for 10 seconds before 2.4 GHz, and one Wi-Fi device refuses the second of hosting and joining with `HotspotError::Joined` or `JoinError::Hosting`. A join is a volatile NetworkManager profile its user owns, which needs `network-control` and `settings.modify.own`, and hosting also needs `wifi.share.protected`. On 2026-10-13 pippo's AX200 and the Galaxy M52 passed all six joins in one unattended run, each hosting in turn while the other joined by the beacon, the pasted payload and the typed form.

**Built (R76, 2026-10-14), the Mac join.** `connetto-peer-apple` joins on macOS through CoreWLAN, scanning for the offered network every 5 seconds for up to 30 seconds since an iPhone's Personal Hotspot answers a directed scan only now and then. macOS hands a process without Location Services access scanned networks with no name, so a Mac join answers `JoinError::MissingPermission("Location Services")` until the user allows it through `connetto_peer_apple::hotspot::request_location_access()`. The Mac never hosts. On 2026-10-16 a headless client on the Mac joined the iPhone 15 Pro Max's Personal Hotspot by the typed form, the Bluetooth beacon and the pasted payload in one unattended run.

## The exchange

**Decided (R77).** Each device offers its own pending queue, a hash chain whose newest entry its device key signs at each hand-off, direct exchange only, no relay and no gossip: a camp group on one network is small and fully pairwise. Per peer, a device keeps the highest sequence received, and an exchange is: mutual offer of queue bounds plus the acked frontier plus the retraction log, request from cursor plus one, signed changesets streamed in order, cursor advanced in the same transaction as the apply.

Cleared sequences need no tombstones, with one repair: an acked row reaches a peer through ordinary subscription delivery only where the peer's subscriptions cover it, so each exchange carries the author's acked frontier, and provisional rows at or below it are marked adjudicated and fall under ordinary coverage. Refusal keeps its own signal: a signed retraction log of refused sequences with their `MutationRejectReason`, and a retraction removes the peer's provisional copy. The author is authoritative over its own queue's fate.

Offline authorization is the translated replica policy evaluated with the author's identity bound, through a rebindable evaluator, failing closed on OpenFGA-derived and locally unanswerable rights. Two limits are part of the contract: the answer is computed on the receiver's partial replica, so an absent membership row produces a false deny (fail closed, accepted), and an offline-stale replica can produce a bounded false allow, the same staleness class as certificate revocation lag.

## The provisional tier

**Decided (R77).** Peer-applied rows live in the ordinary replica tables so the application's own SQL sees them, with provenance beside them in the device-private tier keyed by table and key: author identity, author device, received-at, state. Peer applies suspend capture exactly as server patches do, or a peer's write would re-upload as the receiver's own. Eviction spares provisional rows through their own spare clause beside the pending one. The conflict rule is explicit: the receiver's own unsent write is never displaced, any other collision applies last-writer-locally with the displaced author recorded, and server-wins stays reserved for server patches. Server-sourced data writing a key clears its provenance record. The client exposes typed provenance reads and applied, confirmed and retracted-with-cause events; presentation is the application's.

**Aggregates do not follow, stated so nobody expects it (added by the 2026-08-22 review).** Server-computed aggregate values (`LiveValue`) are pushed by the server and freeze while offline, so they reflect neither the device's own unsent writes nor peer-applied provisional rows, and they catch up only when connectivity returns and the server has adjudicated. An application showing counts or sums over provisional data derives them from the rows with its own local queries, which see the provisional tier by construction.

## Media over the link

**Decided (R79), the join point with chapter 18.** Metadata rows travel in the exchange like any rows, thumbnails are ordinary derived files a peer prefetches over the link (instant at their size), and full content is pull-on-demand by chunk hash: the author's device answers after evaluating the requester against its translated policy fail-closed, the receiver verifies against the hashes the signed row carries, re-encrypts under its own key, and stores in the never-exported cache class. The application can additionally mark content for replication to present peers, the survivability half of couriering.

## Field survivability: courier recovery

**Decided (R78), deployment-opt-in, default off.** A lost device's work is recoverable from the camp: the server accepts author-signed changesets from any certified bearer, deduplicated by the same frontier every path consults. The boundary is cryptographic, not trusted. The courier authenticates as itself and can only relay verbatim what the author's device key signed, its range attestation covering each entry's provenance original as well as the chain, so a courier cannot strip or substitute it; its own document rights are never consulted; what a malicious courier can do is withhold (no worse than not couriering, with sequence-gap refusal preventing reordering) and delay (no worse than the author's own delayed reconnect, policy evaluated at apply time). Five constraints ride with the path because it is the first place session identity and write author differ: opt-in default off, a distinct `auth_events` record per couriered application naming courier and author, per-courier metering, refusal of revoked enrolments, and author notification on next login. Signatures carry no trusted timestamp, so acceptance is governed by the account's and enrolment's standing at apply time, with the author's certificate live at delivery, a same-key renewal keeping the author's records and standing.

## The archive

**Decided (R75, R77).** Provisional rows and all peer bookkeeping (provenance, cursors, the retraction log) travel in the R26/R56 archive because they live in the tiers it already carries, so a restored device resumes exchanges correctly. Peer-fetched cache chunks stay out, and the archive is not the survivability mechanism, the courier path is. The one archive rule the frontier adds is that an import retires the archived writer, an enrolment or a number, and the importing replica writes under a fresh one.
